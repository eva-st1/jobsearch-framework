## Voice discovery

Build a voice model from observed choices, not personality stereotypes.

For each representative sample, note:

- purpose and audience;
- formal, conversational, technical, reflective, or mixed register;
- typical sentence length and rhythm;
- use of questions, fragments, parentheticals, lists, humor, and metaphor;
- degree of certainty and emotional openness;
- favored words or transitions and phrases the author avoids;
- how the author opens, supports a point, and closes.

Summarize only traits that recur across samples. Attach confidence (`high`, `medium`, or `low`) and evidence to each trait. A sample written for a formal application should not automatically govern a personal reflection.

Use the voice model to choose among truthful phrasings. Never use it to infer the author's beliefs, experiences, or claims.

## Angle selection

Score candidate angles informally on four questions:

1. Does the intended reader have this problem or curiosity now?
2. Can the author add experience, analysis, or synthesis that generic search results cannot?
3. Is the central claim specific enough to disagree with or apply?
4. Can the article give the reader a concrete next step?

Prefer a narrower, defensible angle over a broad trend summary. Useful sources of angles include a counterintuitive lesson, a repeated mistake, a before-and-after decision, a tension in current research, a practical framework, or an honest field note from a career transition.

## AIDA for thought leadership

AIDA describes the reader's movement, not four mandatory blocks.

### Attention

Create a promise or tension that the article can fulfill. Favor specificity over superlatives. The title and first paragraph should agree; do not withhold essential context merely to force a click.

### Interest

Move quickly from hook to relevance. Name the stakes, explain what changed, or show the moment that created the insight. Use research to clarify the problem, not to decorate the article with statistics.

### Desire

In professional writing, desire often means wanting a capability, clearer decision, reduced risk, or more useful way to think. Make the better state tangible. Show a mechanism, example, tradeoff, or small proof point.

### Action

Match the request to the relationship and the article. A new reader may reasonably be asked to test one practice or contribute an example. Avoid vague “thoughts?” endings and coercive engagement prompts. A closing can be complete without a question.

## Article structures

Choose the structure that best serves the idea:

- **Argument:** observation → prevailing assumption → evidence → revised view → implications.
- **Field note:** situation → surprise → interpretation → transferable lesson → next experiment.
- **How-to:** costly problem → principles → steps → constraints → first action.
- **Case study:** context → decision → result → limitations → lessons.
- **Trend analysis:** signal → evidence → competing explanations → author's view → what to watch.

Apply the AIDA reader journey within the chosen structure rather than forcing every piece into the same outline.

## Revision scorecard

Score each dimension from 0 to 5 and revise any dimension below 4:

- **Truth:** Every factual claim is supported, attributed, or clearly framed as opinion.
- **Originality:** The article contains a defensible perspective, synthesis, or lived detail.
- **Usefulness:** The reader gains a decision, method, example, or question worth keeping.
- **AIDA flow:** The opening earns attention, the middle deepens value, and the ending enables action.
- **Voice fidelity:** The prose matches the author's demonstrated choices without caricature.
- **Specificity:** Concrete language replaces abstractions and generic professional advice.
- **Restraint:** No clickbait, false certainty, empty inspiration, excessive emojis, or formulaic AI cadence.
- **Readability:** The title, subheads, paragraphs, and transitions support scanning and sustained reading.

## Interview protocol

Interview only to close consequential gaps. Start with available evidence and ask concise questions in rounds of no more than three.

First round when voice is unknown:

1. Share two to five pieces you wrote that feel like you; they can be messages, posts, articles, or notes.
2. Who is the reader you most want to reach?
3. What should the article never sound like?

Second round when the angle lacks evidence:

1. What did you personally observe, decide, or learn about this topic?
2. Which details are public and safe to share?
3. What do you want the reader to think, feel, or do differently?

Reflect the proposed voice card and thesis back to the user when confidence is low or the topic is sensitive. Ask for confirmation before incorporating a newly supplied career claim into a final draft.
